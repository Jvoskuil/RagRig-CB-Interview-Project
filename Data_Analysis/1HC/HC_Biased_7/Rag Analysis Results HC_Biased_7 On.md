**Q1:** Interviewer: Thanks for making time for this. Just to confirm, this conversation is for our internal review of discharge planning practices, it's confidential, and you can skip anything you're not comfortable discussing. Can you tell me your role and how long you've been doing discharge planning? Participant: Sure. I'm a social worker on the internal medicine unit, I've been doing discharge planning for about six years now. I work with a pretty steady caseload, usually eight to twelve active patients at a time. Interviewer: Great. I'd like to walk through a specific case — the one involving Mr. Hoffmann. Can you give me the general shape of what happened? Participant: Yeah, this was a Thursday-into-Friday case, which already tells you something because bed management wants those rooms turned over by Friday noon if at all possible. Mr. Hoffmann is seventy-eight, came in after a fall at home, and he's got COPD on top of that. He lives alone. His daughter's about two hours away, so she's not a daily presence. My job was to figure out, does he go home with some outpatient support, or does he need a short-term rehab stay first. The tricky part was time — we had maybe a day and a half to sort it out before the bed pressure really kicked in. Interviewer: What was the referral information you had going in? Participant: The ED note said he ambulates independently with a cane and lives alone. That's a pretty common profile for us, so I had a sense of where this was probably headed. I went and talked to him myself before PT had even gotten to him — just a short bedside conversation, maybe ten minutes. He was alert, joking around a little, said he was 'fine, just tripped on a rug.' Honestly, within that conversation I felt like I had a good read on him. You develop an instinct for who's steady and who isn't, and my instinct said steady. Interviewer: What happened after that first conversation? Participant: PT saw him later that day. Their formal numbers showed slower gait speed and weaker grip strength than what the ED note suggested. And nursing flagged two near-falls overnight, unwitnessed, so we don't know exactly what happened, but he was found close to the bed rail both times. I noted that, but at that point I'd already sort of built my plan around the independent-living picture, so those things registered more as things to keep an eye on rather than something that should make me start over. Interviewer: Let's slow down on that moment. What were you weighing when you formed that initial impression? Participant: Mostly the ED documentation, since that's the first thing I see, and then my own impression from talking to him. He was sharp, engaged, using the cane fine getting to the bathroom with the nurse. I remember feeling pretty confident, actually — like, within a few minutes of talking to him I felt I had him pegged as someone who could probably manage independently. Interviewer: And when the near-fall notes came in overnight, did that change your read? Participant: Not dramatically, no. I read it more as an overnight fluke — new environment, maybe he got tangled in the call light cord, that kind of thing. The original picture, ambulates independently with a cane, that was still sort of my anchor point for how I was thinking about him. I did mention the near-falls in my note, but I framed the plan around the baseline description rather than treating the new information as something that should reset my assessment. Interviewer: Moving to the next stage — figuring out the discharge destination itself. What information did you have about the daughter's ability to help? Participant: Early on, I called her, and she said she wants to help however she can. That was basically it at that point — no specific days, no specifics on her schedule. But from that, plus the fact he'd gone home successfully after two previous admissions, I moved forward planning for home with outpatient services. Interviewer: What did your follow-up conversations with her and with home care focus on? Participant: Honestly, mostly logistics — when could services start, what days would work best for a home health aide, whether she could be there for the first visit. I was asking more 'how do we make this work' than 'can this actually work.' In hindsight, I didn't really ask her directly whether she could commit to a regular schedule until later. Interviewer: What made you interpret her comment that way initially? Participant: I think it's just — in my experience, families usually do step up more than the first phone call suggests. That's usually how it goes. People say vague things on a first call because they're stressed, and then it works out once services are in place. So I took her willingness at face value as enough to build around. Interviewer: Did that assumption hold up? Participant: Not entirely. A follow-up call later revealed she can only visit twice a week and works full-time. And the home care agency flagged that weekend coverage wasn't guaranteed. Neither of those was shocking exactly, but it did mean the coverage picture was thinner than what I'd been planning around. Interviewer: Let's go to the chart review and risk assessment. Walk me through that. Participant: His chart showed two hospitalizations in the past year, both discharged home fine. No documented falls-at-home readmission for him specifically. But — and this is what really stuck with me — I'd personally handled a case last month, a patient with a pretty similar profile, who was readmitted after a fall at home. That case was still fresh, and honestly it's the first thing that came to mind when I was thinking through Hoffmann's risk, even before I'd looked closely at his own numbers. Interviewer: Did you use the discharge risk-scoring tool at that point? Participant: Not right away. It's available, but at that point I already had a working plan in my head, and it felt more like a box to check than something that would change the direction. I figured I'd get to it, but running it wasn't the thing that felt urgent — moving the plan forward did. If I'd run it right then and it had come back high, I'd have been the one who had to unwind everything and explain the delay to bed management and to him. Just not running it yet didn't feel like I was actually doing anything one way or the other — the plan was already moving on its own, so leaving the tool for later felt like the lower-stakes choice. Interviewer: What did the fuller chart review and the tool eventually show? Participant: When I did look closer, his actual readmission history was lower than what my gut had been telling me. And the tool rated him moderate, not high. So the numbers didn't fully back up how worried I'd been feeling based on that other case. Interviewer: Let's talk about the final decision — finalizing the plan. What were the options at that point? Participant: There was a short-term rehab bed available, but it needed one more day for insurance pre-authorization. The alternative was finalizing home discharge that same day. Bed management wanted an answer. And Mr. Hoffmann himself was pretty upset at the idea of any delay or of going anywhere other than home. Interviewer: What did you think that extra day would actually do to him? Participant: Honestly, I pictured him getting more worked up the longer it dragged on — maybe starting to feel like we'd gone back on our word, losing some trust in the plan altogether. When I mentioned it might just be the one night, for the safety check, he actually said he supposed he could manage if he really had to, even though he clearly didn't like the idea. But even hearing that, it still felt to me like it wouldn't just be one rough night for him — like the frustration and the sense of being stuck here would carry over past the delay itself. So finishing the extra checks felt like it would cost more than it was worth if it meant putting him through that. Interviewer: What tipped it toward same-day discharge? Participant: Honestly, a big piece of it was him. He was distressed even talking about waiting a day, and I didn't want to put him through that when we already had a workable home plan. The extra day would've let us finish the home safety evaluation and firm up the caregiver picture, but weighing that against how upset he clearly was about delay, I leaned toward getting him home that day. Interviewer: What came out of the home safety evaluation once it was finally done? Participant: After discharge, it turned up a loose stair rail and poor lighting in the bathroom. And the daughter later said she felt underprepared for how much support he actually needed. Interviewer: Looking back, if the PT assessment had come in before your first conversation with him, do you think anything would have gone differently? Participant: Maybe. I might have gone in with more questions instead of forming an opinion so quickly. Interviewer: If you hadn't had that other readmission case fresh in mind, would your risk read have been different? Participant: Possibly less urgent-feeling, yeah. It's hard to separate that out completely. Interviewer: Any point where slowing down might have changed the outcome? Participant: Probably the caregiver piece. If I'd pushed harder on specifics with the daughter earlier instead of assuming it would work out, we might have caught the coverage gap sooner. Interviewer: What would you do differently with a similar case tomorrow? Participant: Probably run the risk tool earlier, and ask more direct questions about caregiving capacity right from the first call, rather than filling in the gaps myself.
