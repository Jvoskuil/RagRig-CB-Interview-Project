**Q1:** Interviewer: Thanks for making time for this. Just to confirm, this is a confidential discussion about how you approached a specific staffing decision last term — nothing you say here goes into any personnel file, and you can decline to answer anything. That work for you? Participant: Yes, that's fine. Happy to walk through it. Interviewer: Great. Can you start by telling me your role and what you were responsible for deciding during that period? Participant: I chair the Communications Department. Every spring we run the adjunct renewal cycle — deciding which non-tenure-track instructors get contracts for the following fall. That term it landed right on top of our accreditation self-study, plus the curriculum committee was finishing a two-year review of our capstone sequence. So I had three processes converging in about a month, all needing sign-off from me. Interviewer: What made this cycle different from previous ones? Participant: Normally renewal is routine — you look at the evaluation trend, check for any complaints, and move on. This time one of our longer-tenured adjuncts, who'd taught the intro public speaking sequence for years with strong numbers, had a rockier semester. And it collided with the committee wanting to repurpose his teaching line for a new elective. So instead of one clean decision, it turned into four separate calls over about six weeks. Interviewer: Let's get the whole account first, and then we'll go back through it step by step. What happened? Participant: HR gave us 48 hours to submit the renewal list, which is tight, but it's not unusual — everyone's juggling the self-study at the same time. This instructor had three straight years averaging 4.6 out of 5 on his teaching evaluations, so on paper he was one of our strongest adjuncts. Only two of his four sections had scores in yet, and those were down to 3.9. I had to decide whether to hold the list for the missing two sections or go with what I had. A week after I submitted him for renewal, the other two sections came in lower still — 3.6 average — and around the same time I got a batch of complaint emails, five students, all from one section, saying his lectures felt disorganized and his grading was inconsistent. That same week he'd pushed back pretty hard on a rubric change I'd proposed at a faculty meeting — raised his voice a bit, which isn't like him. I looked at the roster for the complaining section and noticed most of those students had come in through our new transfer-pathway cohort. Meanwhile his other three sections had zero complaints. Then the curriculum committee, which had been building toward 'modernizing' the capstone for two years, proposed folding his line into a new elective a senior colleague wanted to launch. I had a peer-observation memo calling his classroom management 'adequate but inconsistent,' and around the same time an external accreditation reviewer who'd sat in on one of his sessions wrote that it was 'well-structured and responsive.' I ended up backing the reallocation. Finally, three weeks before the HR deadline, with no replacement lined up, I had to decide renew or not. I'd had two one-on-one meetings with him that term and both felt defensive on his end. I went with non-renewal and had to write the Dean a short memo justifying it. Interviewer: Let's reconstruct the order precisely. What did you know before you made the first call, versus what came in afterward? Participant: Before: the three-year average, the two-section partial data, the deadline. After: the other two sections' scores, and separately, the complaint batch. The meeting blow-up and the roster detail came in around the same window as the complaints. The peer memo and the accreditation report landed close together, right before the committee vote. And the 'defensive' meetings happened gradually across the term, so by the time of the final decision that impression was already fully formed in my head. Interviewer: On that first decision — submitting him for renewal with only half the current-term data — walk me through your reasoning. Participant: The deadline was real, 48 hours, and I had the self-study eating the rest of my week. Three years at 4.6 is a strong track record, and 3.9 on two sections isn't alarming by itself — it's still solidly good. My read was that the missing two sections were unlikely to change the overall picture much, so I submitted him. Interviewer: Did you consider asking for even a short extension, or checking in with the instructor or students directly before the deadline? Participant: I thought about emailing HR for two more days, yeah. I didn't, mostly because I assumed it wouldn't move the needle — his history was so consistent. In hindsight I could have just called the two sections' students directly, that would've taken twenty minutes, but with the self-study deadline stacked on top I didn't prioritize it. Interviewer: When the complaints and the meeting incident arrived close together, how did you interpret them? Participant: Honestly, the meeting was fresh in my mind — he'd been unusually sharp with me, out of character — and when the complaint emails came in days later describing 'disorganized' and 'unfair,' it read to me like confirmation that something had shifted with him generally, not just in one classroom. I didn't have anything connecting the meeting tone to his actual teaching that term, but it felt like part of the same pattern. Interviewer: Separately, you mentioned the roster composition. How did that factor in? Participant: Right — when I saw most of the complainants were from the transfer-pathway cohort, my sense was that group tends to be more vocal about grading generally, so I weighted the complaints a bit less as a reflection of his teaching specifically. I didn't actually check whether that cohort files more complaints in other courses; it was more of an impression from prior semesters. Interviewer: What alternative did you rule out there? Participant: I could have gone section by section, talked to a TA, looked at whether other instructors teaching that same cohort saw similar patterns. I didn't do that before forming my view. Interviewer: Move to the committee decision on the teaching line. What tipped you toward reallocation? Participant: The committee had been pushing capstone modernization for two years, and by that point I had the complaints, the meeting tension, and now a peer memo calling his management 'inconsistent.' It all fit together into a fairly clear story — declining performance, aligned with a change the department already wanted to make. The accreditation reviewer's report was more positive, but reviewers usually only see one session, so I gave that less weight. Actually, if I'm honest, the peer memo came from a single sit-in too — one class period — but I read 'inconsistent' as more representative of what was really going on, since it lined up with what I was already hearing from the complaints. Interviewer: The enrollment numbers for the capstone had actually grown 8% that year. Did that come up in your reasoning? Participant: It's in the file, but no, I didn't really fold it into the recommendation. The narrative we were building was about quality and modernization, not enrollment, so it didn't feel central at the time. Interviewer: Last decision point — the final renewal call under the three-week deadline, with no replacement identified. What drove that? Participant: There was a real cost to leaving it unresolved — HR needed an answer, and dragging it out would've meant scrambling even harder later. I could have extended him short-term while we did a fuller review, but I wanted the question closed so I could focus on the self-study. When I wrote the memo to the Dean, I mostly drew on my two meetings with him — I'd sat across from him myself both times, and that felt like a more reliable read on where he really stood than notes filtered through a TA or a peer observer who'd only caught one class. So that's what I led with. Interviewer: How confident were you in that final call, on a gut level? Participant: Moderately. I knew the file had more in it than what I used, but I trusted my own sense of him over those secondhand accounts, so I didn't go back and weigh the TA or peer material more heavily. Interviewer: A couple of hypotheticals. If the missing two sections' scores had been available before your first decision, would anything have changed? Participant: Possibly — if I'd seen 3.6 upfront instead of after, I might have paused the renewal rather than approving it outright. Interviewer: If the accreditation report had reached the committee before the vote instead of after, do you think the outcome shifts? Participant: Maybe. It might have at least prompted a conversation instead of being filed quietly. Interviewer: And if you'd had a vetted replacement ready to go, would the final decision have looked different? Participant: I think I still lean non-renewal given everything by that point, but I probably would've extended him for a term instead of cutting it off right there. The absence of a replacement pushed me toward closing it out faster than I might have otherwise. Interviewer: That's really helpful — thank you for walking through all of that in detail.
